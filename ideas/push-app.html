An app with push notifications

Turn the website into an app that taps students on the shoulder, and decide very carefully when it is allowed to tap.

  • Kind of work: interaction design, user research, front-end, and a small but real piece of back-end
  • You'd enjoy this if: you like testing on real phones, and you care about the eight words on a lock screen as much as the code that sends them

The idea in two minutes. Sound on, captions included.

Why this is worth doing

The site knows a lot that a student would like to hear about: a lecture that moved, a deadline on Friday, a classmate waiting for a review, a slot that must be picked for a design review. Today the student only finds out by opening the site and looking in the right place. The students who look often are fine. The ones who forget are the ones who needed the reminder.

A phone on the table is where a student's attention already is. If the school's site can put one short, useful line there at the right moment, fewer things get missed, and teachers repeat themselves less.

The technical part is smaller than it sounds. A website can become an installable app and send push notifications without an app store, and the tools are free. The hard part is design: getting a student to install it, asking for permission at a moment when "yes" makes sense, and sending so little that nobody turns it off. That is why this is a good project for designers who code.

Where we are today

The site sends nothing. I found no push code, no e-mail sending, no calendar feed and no scheduled job in the project. Everything a student sees, they see because they opened a page.

The site is also not installable yet. There is no web app manifest and no service worker, and the only icon is a small generated favicon with a "V". The good news is that the project runs on Next.js 16, and the official Next.js guide for web apps with push ships inside the project's own node_modules.

What the site already knows is the promising part:

  • The calendar stores events with a category (lecture, deadline, milestone, group work, meeting, holiday) and with a visibility (everyone, one team, shared with named people, private). It also shows group project dates, sign-up sheets, "find a time" polls and bookable meeting hours. It already works out which sheets and polls are waiting for an answer from the person looking.
  • The student home page already has "Give Reviews" (guides where a classmate waits for your review), "Continue Learning" (your next guide), grades per module and a group project widget. So "something needs you" is already calculated. It is only shown, never sent.
  • Sign-in is e-mail and password, and a session ends after about a week without a visit. A student who taps a notification after a quiet holiday will land on the sign-in page. Somebody has to design that moment.
  • Canvas can open the site inside a frame through an LTI launch. That matters because Chrome and Firefox do not allow a page inside a cross-origin frame to ask for notification permission. The install and the permission question have to happen on the site itself, in its own tab.

One more detail for whoever builds the scheduler: calendar events store their date and time as plain school-local text, with no time zone. That was done on purpose to keep holidays on the right day. Anything that says "starts in 30 minutes" must decide what clock it uses.

What the research says

The platform facts below change every year, so I checked each one in November 2026. The human facts change much more slowly.

On an iPhone, push only works after "Add to Home Screen"

Since iOS 16.4 a web app can send push, but only once it has been added to the Home Screen, and it may only ask for permission after a tap. You do not need a paid Apple developer account. For you this means the install step is the whole funnel on iPhone: no install, no push.

Source: WebKit, Web Push for Web Apps on iOS and iPadOS
Since iOS 26, every site added to the Home Screen opens as an app

Apple removed its own requirements: any website a person adds to the Home Screen now opens as a web app by default. What Safari still does not have is an install prompt. Nothing will offer the install for you, so your own onboarding screen has to explain Share, then Add to Home Screen.

Source: WebKit, Features in Safari 26.0
Safari can now show a push without running your code

Declarative Web Push (iOS and iPadOS 18.4, then macOS) lets the server send a small standard JSON message that the browser shows by itself. WebKit built it because service worker push is fragile: a bug or a bad network can stop the notification, and the browser may then cancel the subscription. The same message still works in older browsers through a service worker, so you can support both.

Source: WebKit, Meet Declarative Web Push
Less than 1% of web notifications get any reaction

That is Chrome's own number. Chrome now removes the permission from sites that send a lot and get ignored. Installed web apps are not touched by that clean-up, which is one more reason to get the install right. It is also a warning: the normal fate of a notification is to be ignored.

Source: Google, Reducing notification overload in Chrome (October 2025)
Three batches a day beat both "instant" and "nothing"

In a field experiment with 237 people, those who got their notifications in three batches a day felt more attentive, more productive and more in control of their phone. Those who got no notifications at all got few of those benefits and felt more anxious about missing out. For a school, this is a strong argument for a morning summary and against sending every event the second it happens.

Source: Fitz and others, Computers in Human Behavior, 2019
Asking on the first visit is the worst moment to ask

Google's guidance is to ask when the benefit is obvious, and to show your own explanation first, so that a "not now" does not become a permanent block in the browser. A good moment on this site could be right after a student books a meeting: "Want a reminder before it starts?"

Source: web.dev, Permission UX
On Vercel's free plan, a scheduled job runs once a day, at roughly the right hour

Hobby projects may run each cron job once per day, and it can start up to 59 minutes late. Pro allows every minute. Cron times are always in UTC, which is also Iceland's time all year. A daily summary fits the free plan. "Your lecture starts in 15 minutes" does not, unless you add another scheduler. Find out which plan the school is on before you design around timing.

Source: Vercel, Usage and Pricing for Cron Jobs

How it could be built

1. Installable web app with your own Web Push

Add a manifest, proper icons and a small service worker, as the Next.js guide shows. Create a pair of VAPID keys, save each student's push subscription in MongoDB, and send with the web-push library from a server action or a scheduled route. Use the Badging API to put a number on the app icon for "reviews waiting". Send the Declarative Web Push format so that Safari can show the message by itself.

Free, no store, no review, and student data stays in the school's own database. One codebase covers iPhone, Android and laptops. Updates go live with a normal deploy.

Every iPhone student has to add the site to the Home Screen by hand before anything works. Subscriptions stop working when someone changes phone or removes the app, so you need to clean up the old ones. If you want minute-exact reminders on the free Vercel plan you need a second scheduler, for example Upstash QStash, whose free tier allows 1,000 messages a day.

2. Notifications first, the app second

Build the part that decides before the part that delivers: one list of events worth telling (review waiting, event changed, slot to pick), a settings page per student, and an "outbox" that records what was sent. Then add channels one at a time. The cheapest first channel is a personal calendar feed (an .ics address made with ical-generator) that students subscribe to in the calendar app they already use. The second could be a morning e-mail through Resend, whose free plan covers 3,000 e-mails a month and 100 a day. Web Push becomes channel three.

It reaches the students who will never install anything. The design work (what to send, when, how to turn it off) is the same for every channel, so nothing is wasted. A calendar feed needs no permission and no scheduler at all.

Calendar apps decide themselves how often they fetch a feed, so a feed is for planning and cannot say "in 15 minutes". A feed address is a secret link to a personal schedule and needs a way to be reset. E-mail from a school address needs somebody to set up the domain.

3. A notification service does the sending

Keep the web app from option 1, but let a service hold the subscriptions, the per-student preferences and the retries. OneSignal has a free plan with web push and 10,000 e-mails a month. Knock (10,000 messages a month free) and Novu (10,000 workflow runs a month free) add ready-made preference screens and digests. Firebase Cloud Messaging covers web, Android and Apple with one API.

Digests, quiet hours and "send by push, fall back to e-mail" already exist, so you spend the weeks on design. The free tiers should be enough for one school.

Student names, e-mail addresses and activity leave the school's database and go to an outside company. Someone at the school has to agree to that, and you have to read where the data is stored. You also learn less about how push works, and the iPhone install step stays exactly as hard.

4. A real app in the stores

Wrap the site with Capacitor, or rebuild the student screens with Expo and React Native. Push then goes through Apple's and Google's own systems.

Students find it in the store, install it like any app, and never hear the words "Add to Home Screen". Push on iPhone is as reliable as it gets.

Apple's developer programme costs $99 a year and Google Play takes $25 once. A new personal Google account must first run a closed test with at least 12 testers for 14 days. Apple's review guideline 4.2 says an app must be more than "a repackaged website", and a wrapped site is exactly what reviewers look for. Every update waits for review. For one school this is a lot of ceremony.

A first week

  1. Put three devices on the table An iPhone, an Android phone and a laptop. Install whatpwacando.today on each and turn on its notifications. Record the screen. Count the taps and write down every screen where you had to think.
  2. Ask five students two questions "What did you miss this term, and how did you find out?" and "Which apps are allowed to send you notifications, and which did you turn off?" Their answers are your list of features and your list of mistakes to avoid.
  3. List every possible notification Go through the calendar, the student home page and the group pages. For each thing the site knows, write the notification as it would look on a lock screen, eight words at most. Sort the list by "I would be glad" and "I would be annoyed". Keep three.
  4. Send one push to your own phone On a branch, follow the Next.js guide: manifest, icons, service worker, VAPID keys, one test button. Use a Vercel preview address, because push needs HTTPS. Test on the iPhone early. It has the most rules.
  5. Draw the two difficult moments The install explanation for an iPhone, and the permission question. Draw the "no" path as carefully as the "yes" path: what does a student who said no see next week?
  6. Decide on timing, then write one page Find out which Vercel plan the school uses. Choose between a morning summary and exact reminders. End the week with a one-page proposal: three notifications, one channel, one way to measure whether it helped.

Still open

  • Who is allowed to make thirty phones buzz? Only the system, when something changes in the calendar, or also a teacher who types a message? What stops that from becoming the school's loudest channel?
  • If half the class installs the app and half does not, is that a success? What do the others get: an e-mail, a calendar feed, or nothing?
  • Should a grade or teacher feedback arrive as a push? It can show on a lock screen in front of friends. Is "You have new feedback" enough, or is even that too much?
  • One summary in the morning, or each thing when it happens? Does the student choose, or do you choose for them and defend it?
  • Do teachers get notifications too, for example when a meeting is booked or a return waits for grading? Their needs and their patience are different from the students'.
  • A sign-in lasts about a week. Should the installed app keep a student signed in for longer, so that a tap on a notification always opens the right page? What is the risk on a shared or lost phone?
  • How will you know it worked? Fewer late reviews, fewer empty seats, or simply fewer students who switched it off after a month?
