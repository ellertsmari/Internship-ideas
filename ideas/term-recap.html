Your term, looked back on

A personal page at the end of each term that is worth sharing, and that compares you only with yourself a few months ago.

  • Kind of work: data storytelling, motion and card design, Next.js, and some careful thinking about privacy
  • You'd enjoy this if: you like turning rows in a database into one sentence that somebody wants to screenshot

The idea in two minutes. Sound on, captions included.

Why this is worth doing

A student at Vefskólinn returns guides, reviews classmates, gets reviewed, builds group projects and presents them. The site records all of it. Then the term ends and none of it is shown back to the student as a story. They see a progress bar and a list of what is still missing.

A recap turns the same records around. In September you had never deployed a site. By December you returned this many guides, wrote this many reviews, one classmate wrote this about your work, and here is the project you presented. The student sees how far they have come, in their own work, with no ranking against anybody.

Three groups get something. Students get proof of progress at the moment they are tired. The school gets pages that students choose to post, which shows real student work to people who might apply. And you get a design problem with a hard centre: the page must be honest and kind for a student who had a bad term, and still be something a strong student is proud to share.

On the wall this idea sits next to The student just ahead of you. That one uses other students to motivate you. This one is its gentler cousin: it uses the same activity data and compares you only with you. The two could share one data layer and show two faces.

Where we are today

The raw material exists already. Every return has a date, a project name, a link to the code, a link to the live version, an optional picture and the student's own comment. Every peer review has a date, a written comment and a vote: no pass, pass, or recommend to the Hall of Fame. Teachers grade the reviews. Guides belong to modules, and the auto-graded exercise guides keep a score.

Group projects keep the team, the project name and description, links, a team photo and a presentation day. Teammates score each other and write a comment. A student can read the written feedback once they have handed in their own evaluations, and grades wait until a teacher releases them. The calendar knows what a term is: teachers set a label, a start date and an end date.

What a student sees of this is about the present. The home page shows progress overall and per module, three counts (passed, Hall of Fame, returned) and an empty space reserved for a leaderboard. Teachers have a Reports page where they pick a student and see progress and review activity. The Hall of Fame and the public showcase show finished work.

Nothing looks back. No page puts a student's term in order, nothing compares the start of a term with its end, and nothing is made to be shared by one student about their own work. One useful detail: the site already uses the Next.js ImageResponse API in one small place (the site icon), so the tool for drawing share images is installed.

What the research says

The research is friendly to this idea in some places and a clear warning in others. Read the warnings first.

A dashboard alone does not help students

A 2023 review of 38 studies of student-facing learning dashboards found no evidence that they improve achievement, and only modest effects on motivation. Showing numbers is not enough. The recap has to say what the numbers mean.

Source: Kaliisa and others, "Have Learning Analytics Dashboards Lived Up to the Hype?"
Small steps are what people feel

Amabile and Kramer read about 12,000 daily diaries from 238 employees. Progress in work that matters was the strongest lift, and small wins worked almost as well as big ones. A recap is a machine for making small wins visible.

Source: Harvard Business School Working Knowledge on The Progress Principle
Writing a reflection beat more practice

Trainees who spent the last 15 minutes of the day writing about what they learned scored 22.8 percent higher on the final test than those who kept working. So ask a question before you show a number. One caution: this is a single study, so treat the exact figure with care.

Source: HBS Working Knowledge on "Learning by Thinking" (Di Stefano, Gino, Pisano, Staats)
The best comparison depends on the student

In two e-learning experiments (203 and 132 people), feedback that compared learners with their own past or with others worked best when it matched what the learner preferred. This is the argument for building this idea and the gamification idea as a pair, and letting students lean to one side.

Source: Janson, Siebert and Dickhäuser, 2022
Share cards are made by the top ten percent

Duolingo found that the top 10% of learners produced more than half of all shares of its Year in Review. Their answer was "learner styles": a card about how you learn, which every learner can get. Your recap needs a card like that for the middle of the class.

Source: Duolingo, "How we built a personalized Year-in-Review"
A cheerful recap can hurt

In 2014 Facebook's Year in Review showed Eric Meyer a photo of his daughter, who had died that year, under the line "here's what your year looked like!" His advice to designers: ask before you show, and design for the worst case first. A student who failed a module or was ill is your worst case.

Source: Eric Meyer, "Inadvertent Algorithmic Cruelty"
People want their own numbers, in detail

Spotify's 2024 Wrapped was criticised for giving fewer detailed stats. The 2025 edition reached 200 million users in 24 hours, where 2024 needed 62 hours, and was shared more than 500 million times. Specific and personal wins over clever and general.

Source: TechCrunch, December 2025

How it could be built

A story page from live data

One route in the Next.js app. On the server it reads the student's returns, reviews and group work between the term's start and end dates, and builds six to eight cards. On the phone the cards are a tap-through story made with CSS scroll snap. Motion comes from scroll-driven animations and React's ViewTransition, with canvas-confetti (ISC licence) for the last card.

No new database collection, no new service, no cost. You can show a working recap with real data in the first two weeks.

The numbers change if somebody returns a guide late or a review is edited. Scroll-driven animations arrived in Chrome first and much later in Safari and Firefox, so the page must read well without them. canvas-confetti ignores "reduce motion" unless you switch on disableForReducedMotion.

A saved snapshot that the student edits

When the term ends, compute each recap once and save it as one document in MongoDB. The student opens a draft, picks which feedback quote to show, hides any card they do not like, and then publishes. The saved recap stays the same for good and can later appear in the portfolio you are building.

The student is in control, which solves most privacy and kindness problems. Old recaps do not break when the site's data model changes. Comparing this term with the last one becomes easy.

You must decide the freezing moment, and what happens to work returned after it. An editing screen is a second product to design.

Share images with ImageResponse

A route handler that returns a PNG, drawn from JSX with ImageResponse from next/og (it uses Satori underneath). Set width and height for a tall story card. The share button uses navigator.share with the image as a file, and falls back to a download button.

It is built into Next.js and runs on Vercel with no extra service. Works with either option above.

Satori supports flexbox and a subset of CSS: no grid. The bundle limit is 500KB including fonts, and fonts must be ttf, otf or woff. The Web Share API is not available in every browser, so test with navigator.canShare(). An image URL is public to whoever has it: sign it, and never draw a grade on it.

A video with Remotion

Write the recap as React components with Remotion, play it in the browser with the Remotion Player, and render an MP4 for sharing. GitHub Unwrapped does this with AWS Lambda for rendering.

Video is what gets posted, and the open GitHub Unwrapped code is a complete example to learn from.

Rendering needs AWS and costs money per video. The Remotion licence is free for individuals and companies of up to 3 people; larger organisations pay, with a monthly minimum. Ask whether a school counts before you write a line. Treat this as a second step after a still recap works.

A first week

  1. Take three recaps apart Go through Duolingo's write-up, GitHub Unwrapped and Raycast Wrapped with a notebook. For each screen write the one number it shows, the sentence around it, and what moves. Count the screens.
  2. Write fifteen sentences the data can say Use the list in "Where we are today". For example: "Your first return was on this date." "You reviewed this many classmates." "This project was recommended to the Hall of Fame." Mark each one kind, risky or boring.
  3. Design the hard term first Write the full recap for a student who returned three guides, got one "no pass" and missed weeks. If you can make that page true and kind, the strong student's page is easy. If you cannot, you have found the real project.
  4. Add the question card Before any number, ask one thing: "What are you most proud of this term?" Decide where the answer goes and who can read it. The reflection research says this card may matter more than all the others.
  5. Prototype six cards and one share card In Figma or on paper. Show them to three students and ask two questions: "Is this true about you?" and "Would you post this, and where?"
  6. Do one technical test Build a single route that draws a tall share card with made-up data using ImageResponse, and open it on your phone. Then try the share button. You will learn the limits of Satori in one afternoon.

Still open

  • Does a student with almost no activity get a recap? Raycast shows nothing under 14 days of use. Showing nothing is also a message, and so is a nearly empty page.
  • Who chooses "the best feedback you got"? A classmate wrote it for one reader. Can it go on a public card, with or without their name, and do they get a say?
  • What is "a few months ago" for a student in their first term? There is no earlier term to compare with. First month against last month? First return against latest return?
  • Duolingo's percentile card raised sharing, mostly among top learners. Does any comparison with others belong here, or does all of it move to the gamification idea?
  • Should teachers be able to read a student's recap and their written reflection? Students write differently when a teacher is reading.
  • Is it a term, a module or a whole year? And does it arrive on one day for everybody, or build up month by month like Apple Music Replay?
